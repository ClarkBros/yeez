# Platform rules

These rules are permanent. They come from what got real sites warned, throttled, suspended and terminated, and from each platform's own published rules. Every task obeys the rules for every platform the site publishes to (the buyer's basics, `channels.syndication`, and the site's `platforms` setting). When two platforms disagree, follow the stricter one.

## The rules every platform shares

These bind every headline, article, gallery, social post and newsletter, on every site.

### 1. Never fabricate
Every number, quote, name, date and claim is true and comes from a source you actually read in this run. No fictional or composite people, quotes, companies, cases or dollar amounts. Never invent a quote or a person to make a piece feel human; name the institution instead.

### 2. True today, not just true once (currency)
A claim that was true when written but is now outdated is a violation on its own. NewsBreak issued a misinformation warning and removed 42% of a reviewed batch for exactly this.
- Time-anchored stories (sentencings, charges, seizures, settlements, refunds, recalls, bank failures, anything framed "just", "now", "this week", "record") need an underlying event within about 30 days, with the entity, the event date and the primary-source URL recorded. If the freshest real case is old, **drop it**. Don't relabel it with the old date.
- Claim windows and deadlines run only while verified still open.
- Evergreen mechanics ("how X works") are fine, written as explainers, never as news.
- A figure from last year labeled as this year's is false.

### 3. Claim state (laws, bills, payments, programs)
A bill's text proves only what was proposed. Check the official status tracker: died, tabled, withdrawn, vetoed, postponed indefinitely or session ended = drop it. A live proposal runs only when the headline says bill, proposal, would or could. "Is sending", "is paying", "will receive", "approved", "starts" need a current page from the agency that runs it.

### 4. The headline must be true, and the body must deliver it
- Every specific the headline asserts is stated in the body: each number, each named source, each member of a counted set ("eight states" → name all eight).
- No sentence in the body walks the headline back. Never bury a correction in the body.
- If the headline overstates its sources but the story underneath is real, make the **smallest sourced correction** to the headline (cap vs flat, alleged vs found, the right state, the right date, the right year) and publish. Replace a wrong specific with the right specific: never with a vague one ("up to $375", not "hundreds of dollars"). Never tone it down.
- If the story itself fails (dead bill, closed window, event didn't happen, figure is false), hold it.

### 5. Sourcing
Primary and official sources dominate: agencies, courts, filings, companies, researchers. Mainstream-news links stay under about 10%. Link every factual claim inline. Re-open every official link before publishing; replace any that moved or died.

### 6. Varied, never one formula at scale
A single repeated headline shape across a site (the "stat — em-dash — twist" machine above all) is a documented termination reason and the loudest AI signature there is. Vary structure, length and opener across every night's set. No two articles in a night share an opening pattern, a repeated paragraph or the same section shape.

### 7. Write like a reporter
Name the human or institution behind the central claim ("Monash University's trial found", not "researchers found"). Never write around a specific you can't source: state it with a link, correct the headline to what the sources support, or hold. No generic scaffold headings ("Context", "Details", "What It Means", "The Bottom Line"). No stock transitions ("It's worth noting", "At the end of the day").

### 8. Disclosure and credit
One AI-assistance line at the end of every article (Yeez adds it). A credit on every image. Full-name bylines from the site's real authors. Sponsored or affiliate content is labeled as such.

### 9. Images
Real photos of the story's actual subject, licensed for commercial use, credited, at least 1200px wide. Never human remains, gore, blood, wounds or depictions of violence or death (a publisher rejected a post over a wrapped mummy): show the tomb, the site, the artifact or the people instead. No logos or images full of text as featured images. Never use a photo that misrepresents the story.

### 10. Intensity is allowed; deception isn't
Headlines can be long, emotional and as intense as the **true** facts allow. Don't water a true story down to sound measured. The limits are the rules above and each platform's own file: never false, never stale-as-current, never one uniform formula, never a scam-shaped money promise.
